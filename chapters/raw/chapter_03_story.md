On the first Monday of term, David discovered that he was able to cross the campus in eleven minutes if he did not stop to look at the sea. According to his timetable, mathematics began at eight in the old science building, and he arrived early. The classroom smelled of chalk and rain. The professor, a thin man with a rapid way of speaking, filled the board with equations and assigned sixteen problems for Wednesday. "The curriculum is a ladder," he said. "Every step depends on the one below it." David followed almost all of it. After the lecture, a boy argued that the last proof was wrong; the professor added a line to the board. "You are right," he said. The chemistry hour was harder. The teacher drew a carbon compound and asked what it was used for. When nobody spoke, she dissolved a spoon of white powder in water and held the glass up. "This is in your kitchen and your medicine," she said, and then illustrated the structure with her hands. At the end she gave them a sheet of safety rules and informed them that anyone who broke the code would lose lab permission.

The English class met in a low room with tall windows. Ms. Chen introduced herself by writing one word on the board: Listen. Her first assignment was an essay, due the following Monday, on a subject of their own choosing. "Try to be honest," she said. "An honest page is worth more than a clever one." David, who had scarcely written an essay before, felt a familiar strain in his chest. That evening he stared at a blank sheet until the lamp's cable grew warm, and he reminded himself of his father's advice: begin with what you can see. He wrote about the view from the window — the memorial garden, the gardener bent over his roses, a jet climbing into the blue. He had made a beginning, and he frowned at the weak middle. Before he could submit it, he would have to make it better.

Friday's lab took place in a basement room that felt like a cave. The demonstrator, an expert with steady hands, showed them how to heat a mineral sample until it glowed. "The color is the character of the substance," she said. David held the tongs and tried not to let his grip shake. The sample cracked, and smoke drifted toward the ceiling. Someone in the back coughed. The demonstrator tapped the bench. "That is a defect in the sample, not in you. Every experiment teaches you something, even the ones that fail; that is the best example." The class laughed, and the tension weakened. By the end of the hour David had begun to enjoy the work. He wrote one line at the bottom of the page: "Ask better questions."

On Wednesday evening Sarah took him to the English Club on the second floor of the student center. The room was lively: sixteen chairs, a worn sofa, and an old typewriter nobody seemed to use. Sarah introduced him to Han, the club's anchor. "We read a poem, and then we argue about it," Han said. "That is the tradition." Han read a poem about a swallow returning over the sea, and his voice was so graceful that even the boys in the back stopped frowning. David did not utter a word, but he listened with his whole body. On the walk home Sarah glanced at him. "You were quiet," she said. "Pardon?" "I was listening," he said. They walked the rest of the way together.

Mark's football trial fell on a sunny Saturday. He had talked about it for a week, and even David knew the formation. Thirty boys turned up, all convinced they were the next great sportsman. The coach divided them into two teams and threw a ball onto the grass. Mark played as if his whole fortune depended on the hour. He kicked, chased, fell, got up, and kicked again. Once his shot hit the crossbar so hard that the crowd sighed. David watched from the sideline, gripping the fence, and felt an old conflict: he wanted Mark to succeed, and he also wanted to be somewhere else. In the last minute Mark scored. The victory was small and sweaty, but he celebrated as if he had won the world cup.

After the trial Mark insisted on cooking dinner in the apartment he shared with David. He called the room an apartment. He fried an egg, added rice, and served it in a blue bowl. "A special meal for a special day," he said. David offered to pay for the pop, and they drank from china cups Mark had bought for almost nothing. The bill came to very little. They ate together, and Mark retold the trial, adding details each time. "The coach said I have a natural tendency to find space," he said, and David laughed. On Thursday Mark came down with a fever and stayed in bed with a fat book. David brought soup and lecture notes, and the fever broke by Saturday. "You are a good nurse," Mark said. "Do not expect me to return the favor," David replied.

The university had assigned each first-year student a study consultant. David's was a quiet girl named Wu, who asked surprisingly direct questions. "How much sleep do you get?" "Six hours." "That is not enough." She helped him build a weekly plan with room for study, the club, and a walk by the sea. He decided to devote one hour each evening to the plan. "The point is to know which hour matters," she said. David followed the plan for two weeks, and the depression that had followed him from the coast began to lift. The baggage of the first month grew lighter. He walked directly to the library after lunch each day, and he was beginning to feel that he might qualify as a real student. The method was not heroic, but it was the only one that worked.

The campus newspaper published a series of short pieces under the heading "Campus Life." One piece called the library the finest institution on the coast. David read it at the counter while the librarian sorted the returns. He browsed the shelves every afternoon, regardless of how much work he had, and could not resist the quiet. The librarian, an expert in old maps, showed him a volume of coastal charts. "Maps prove that the world was always bigger than we thought," she said. He also read about an incident at the east gate: a man had tried to steal bicycles. The paper called him a criminal. The student code was clear, however, and the case was closed. David folded the newspaper and put it in the basket. "Every garden has a defect," the gardener had told him. "You pull the weed and keep the flower." It was humble wisdom, and he carried it throughout the afternoon.

On the first Sunday in October, David and Sarah took a bus to a fishing village beyond the harbor. The road ran through fields of wheat and across a narrow strip of land, then ended at a beach where a few boats lay at anchor. A recent storm had left a dead fish on the sand, and a fat gull was pulling at it. They watched a swallow dip over the water. Sarah, a lover of nature, knew every bird. "That one sails without moving its wings," she said. David listened, but he was thinking of other things. "Do you ever feel that you might fail?" he asked. "Everyone does," she said. "The trick is not to let it become a habit." They ate bread on a rock, and a quiet pleasure spread through him. At the cafe, David browsed an old newspaper. The bus home was crowded, the smog of the city pressed against the glass, and the volume of conversation filled every corner. A child choked on a sweet, and the driver stopped; it was not a real emergency, but everyone stood up.

On Friday the club met again. Han announced that the university had given the club its own room. David agreed to commit two evenings a week to the club's newspaper. "You will be our reporter," Han said. He was not sure the work would fit, but he wanted to try. In November the club staged a short production of a play about a fishing village. The set held a basket, a blue bowl, a rope, and an old anchor. A cable came loose and the lamps went out. Mark stood up and said this was obviously the work of a criminal. Someone fixed it with tape, and the audience laughed. Han said the evening had proved a universal truth: a story could turn a room of strangers into a tribe. Afterwards David helped with the disposal of the props and walked home past a yellow leaf. The domestic life of the dormitory no longer felt strange. He had done almost nothing important, and yet he felt he had won a victory. He submitted his first report the next morning and cut it in half, thereby learning more about writing than in any lecture. The term had barely begun, but he could hear its rhythm. The club had rooted him. If he was patient, patience would prevail. He pressed his ear to the dormitory door, heard Mark snoring, and smiled. He was willing to stay.
