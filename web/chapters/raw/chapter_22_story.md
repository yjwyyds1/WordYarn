The last exam ended on a Friday, and by Saturday the campus was emptying. At the station the queue curled past posters that advertise cheap airline tickets, and the fare boards glowed red. David had done the arithmetic three times: the train fare home, the bus to the village, a gift for his nephew, and the money left for next semester's tuition. It was not enough. "Stay," his mother said on the phone, though her voice was not steady. "Save the fare. We will see you in summer." His father, behind her, said a man should learn to be alone. David said he would be fine, and then sat on his bed for a long time. This would be his first Spring Festival away from home. Mark was staying too, because he had taken a part-time job at a hotel near the station, and Sarah was staying for a design competition. The three of them watched the city grow quiet, its naked branches black against a pale sky.

The university announced a culture week for students who stayed. To prepare, they met in the student center, a busy hall with a mechanical clock above the door. Lin, a tiny junior from the south, was in charge; she was extremely organized and carried a clipboard. "We need twenty volunteers," she said. "Every contribution counts, however slight." They polished the windows, posted notices, and had to dig holes for poles on the lawn, then linked lanterns in a long loop across the ceiling. David held the ladder while Mark climbed. "Whom should I ask about the wiring?" Mark asked. "The woman in the blue coat," Lin said. Inside, the interior was warm; outside, the wind was awful. Besides the lanterns, they arranged chairs in rows and carried trays of tea. The approach was simple: combine work with talk, and the work goes faster.

On Monday evening they made dumplings. Mrs. Zhou, a woman of sixty with quick hands, showed them how to mix the filling, how to roll the dough, and how to weigh each piece so that every dumpling was equal. "Twenty per person," she said. "No more, no less." The oven was already roasting a duck, and a pot of noodle soup bubbled on the stove. David's first dumpling looked like a small plane; his second looked like an elephant. Mark's collapsed into a flat disc, as flat as a coin, and the dough stuck to his palm. "Oh," Sarah said, "that one is a saucer." Everyone laughed. A paper cut on David's finger began to bleed, and Sarah offered to lend him a plaster. Later, a campus cat scratched Mark's hand when he tried to feed it; he forgave the cat at once and stroked its back. By ten o'clock his stomach hurt from tasting, and the tray in front of them held a considerable mountain of dumplings, fresh and uneven. The mixture was simple, but the weight of the work was not.

On Wednesday the lawn became a market. There was an antique stall with a magnetic compass, a brass telescope, old photos and winter clothing; there was a charity table raising money for a children's cancer fund, and a table where pupils from a village school sold postcards. A photography club took a photo of David and Sarah under the bridge of lanterns and said they could collect it after the holiday. David bought a postcard of the old city for his nephew and posted it at the gate. Sarah entered the poster competition and won a small prize; her design, a red lantern over a gray river, was chosen for the program. "Be realistic," a vendor told David when he admired a "historical" bowl. "That one is new." Sarah warned him with a look, and he put the bowl down. The market was a good tour of the city's food, history and industry in one afternoon.

The lantern fair opened on Friday at dusk. The crowd was dense, a slow flow of people across the lawn, and the lanterns glowed in the shapes of a star, an elephant, and a tiny solar system, with a lamp sun and painted planets in orbit around it. A guy from the astronomy club adjusted the telescope, and David looked at the moon until his eye ached. "Gravity keeps it all together," the student said, "the same force that keeps us on the ground." A small boy's lantern caught on a branch and tore; David lifted him so he could undo the string. "I dare you to reach the top one," Mark said, pointing, but David shook his head and laughed. A dog barked somewhere in the dark. The noise was acceptable until midnight, when the fireworks began. The law allowed them only in the park, so the only vehicle inside the gate was the fire truck. The blasts shook the windows, and for a moment the noise was awful; then everyone cheered, and David felt a relief he had not expected. "Goodbye, old year," Sarah said. "Hello," David answered, "to whatever comes next."

On Saturday the hall hosted a show. The drama club performed a short play about a corrupt official and an honest clerk, and about a family after a divorce; the cast was young but confident, and Sarah had helped paint the set. Mark, to everyone's surprise, played a guard in a red suit that did not suit him. He missed a cue, forgot his one line, and improvised something about the weather; the audience laughed, and the ending, which should have been serious, became warm. After the play a teacher gave a short talk on the festival's history and geography, from the wheat north to the rice south, from the autumn harvest to the spring declaration. "Traditions exist because people need them," she said. "They are a bridge between the past and the future, a festival born in farming villages." Her knowledge of local history was impressive, and she spoke about the city's walls and their destruction a century ago. The program, which David had helped draft and edit, listed the duration of each event; the play ran forty minutes, and the talk twenty. When they discussed it afterward, a student objected that the festival had become commercial, and another said the spirit was still there. David thought both of them were partly right.

That evening a rumor spread through the dormitories: the culture week would be cancelled because of an epidemic. The message came through a group chat and grew with each retelling. Lin called the organizers and asked for evidence; the health office said there was none, and the rumor was false. The next morning she posted a plain warning: check the official notice, and do not forward unverified messages. "We should condemn the rumor, not the people who believed it," she said. The most important thing, she added, was to stay calm. The week also brought a small theft: someone took the donation box from the charity table. Campus police had to tackle the case quickly, reviewed the camera footage, and managed to identify a young man who had been hanging around the gate. They went to his rented room, hoping to find the box, and there it was, with most of the money still inside, and they returned the property. The man said he had lost his job and had a child to feed. The organizers decided not to press charges, though the law allowed it. "Punishment is not the only answer," Lin said. "Sometimes it is the wrong one."

On New Year's Eve David called home. His grandmother held the phone too close, so that only her forehead filled the screen; his nephew crawled across the floor behind her, and his parents' marriage of thirty years was visible in the way they stood together without speaking. "We miss you," his mother said. "Eat something warm." Mark and Sarah came over with a plate of dumplings, and the software on Mark's phone played the campus radio. They talked about the future. Sarah had an ambition to study design in the south; Mark wanted to run his family's farm and maybe a hotel of his own one day. David said he wanted to attain a place in the graduate program, and that the semester had given him more motivation than he expected. "You have to be realistic," Mark said, "but realistic does not mean small." They raised cups of tea and said sincere things that would have sounded foolish in daylight. David kept one thought private: he promised himself to visit in summer and to cherish the ordinary evenings. He thought about the past year, and found that he could forgive its wrong turns.

After midnight they walked back through the quiet campus. The stars were clear and cold, and their breath hung in the air. "I did not think I would enjoy staying," David said. "Neither did I," Sarah said. "But here we are." They stopped on the bridge over the small river, where the lanterns were still lit, and watched their reflections shake in the water. David knew the holiday would end, the semester would begin, and the busy city would return. But something in him had shifted. The past was a reference, not a cage; the future required patience, and he had learned to retain the warmth of a good evening against a long winter. He did not know whom he would become, or where he would live, but he was no longer afraid of the question. They said goodnight, and he slept until noon.
