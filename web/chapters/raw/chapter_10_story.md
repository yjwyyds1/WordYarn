The science fair was announced on the first Monday of April with a poster in every dormitory, and students from every department would participate. This year the theme was technology for the community, and the prize was a grant plus a recommendation to a national competition. David read the poster twice on his way across the basketball court, then went up to his room. Mark was sitting on his bed with a letter from his father. It said that the valley had received no rain since February, that the stream behind the village had dried up, and that the sheep were thin. "Nineteen years," Mark said. "My father says it is the worst drought in nineteen years. The animals are drinking from a tank." He tried to smile. "Happy birthday to me." David had forgotten the date. He looked at the poster in his hand, and suddenly the fair had a subject.

The next morning David crossed the east campus to the laboratory and found Sarah at the bench. "We should build something for Mark's village," he said. "An automatic system that waters the fields when the soil is dry. A small pump, a sensor, and a computer." The idea aroused interest, and Sarah liked the scheme at once. They wrote an agenda on the whiteboard: research, design, build, test, present. The bulk of the work would fall on the two of them, because Mark had gone home for the weekend and his absence was already a problem. "He will be back on Tuesday," David said. "He can be our tester." Sarah did not look convinced. "A tester who cannot tell a bolt from a bottle," she said. "First we need to know what dry soil feels like. That is our prime concern, not the prize."

They hunted for parts in the workshop behind the lab. It had a cement floor, a heap of old machines, and an old carpenter's bench. A carpenter from the village agreed to make the frame from light wood, and David reinforced the joints with metal plates. He tied an apron around his waist, cut wires, connected the sensor to a small computer, and tested the pump. The pump needed no operator: when the soil was dry, it started by itself, and the sensor would transfer its reading. The first version was a disaster. The pump was too strong, the tube jumped out, and water went everywhere. Sarah wiped the table with her sleeve and laughed. "It is a good thing we are not being graded on the floor," she said. David injured his elbow on a bolt, and his shirt was wet and his hands were black. They decided to hang the design on the wall.

The laboratory had strict rules, and one of them banned food and drink. Mark, back from the village, did not think the ban applied to tea. He arrived with a flask of tea and a package of peanuts, and he was still pouring when the security guard walked in. "You are violating the rules," the guard said, in a tone that was more tired than angry. "I have told you repeatedly." Mark apologized, and the guard let them off with a warning. The trouble was not over. A team from the business school accused them of copying an idea from a contemporary journal and demanded that the administration act. David disagreed, loudly. "It is a common scheme," he said. "Any rational person can see that we built it ourselves." Sarah, calmer, showed the judge their notebook with every sketch. The accusation died, but the mood in the lab stayed low for two days.

The real battle was with the sensor. It responded to water but not to dry soil, and no amount of adjustment helped. They tested it on specimens from the valley, from the east field, and from a tray of sand that Sarah called the desert. The results were terrible: the pump either flooded the pots or did nothing at all, and the death of a fifth of the seedlings discouraged them. The greenhouse reached thirty-eight degrees, and the heat made everything worse. "We cannot guarantee anything with this," David said. "Maybe the whole idea is ridiculous." Sarah refused to give up. She took the sensor to a colleague of their professor, a skilled engineer, who examined it for an hour and found the problem: a poor connection. He gave them a new part and a recommendation for the fair. The next test showed a significant improvement. By Friday the device was watering a full tray of plants without a single drop on the floor.

Money was the next problem. The grant did not cover the pump, so David went to the administration office, where a clerk in the personnel department explained the forms. The clerk said a stamped form was necessary. The investment was small — a mere five hundred yuan, plus the cost of the frame — but the paperwork took a week. At the market, a salesman tried to sell them a portable pump for twice the price, with a guarantee that was worth nothing. "It is a special model," he said. "Very popular." David asked whether it could run on a battery, and the salesman's answer was unclear. They bought a cheaper pump from a shop near the supermarket instead. The owner did them a favor and threw in a spare tube. "Bring it back if it fails," she said. "I will exchange it."

On the eve of the fair they loaded the device into a van and followed the route to the city museum. Mark steered the van while David held the frame steady. The lobby was crowded, and they had to weave through the crowd and squeeze into a queue before a guard checked their tickets. "Leave your luggage at the desk," he said. Inside, the mayor opened the fair with a short speech about the community, and a chorus of cameras clicked. The mayor's gaze moved across the hall. Near the door stood a famous diamond in a glass case. David and Sarah helped hang their poster behind the table. The setting was grand: high windows and a banner with the names of nineteen teams. Mark arrived late with a birthday cake, and a group of students sang him a song. There was no time to chat. Then the first judge stopped at their table, and his footsteps on the marble sounded like a clock.

Each team had fourteen minutes. David spoke first, and he emphasized the cost: a mere five hundred yuan, plus the pump, plus labor. "A farmer can afford this," he said. "It is not a luxury but a tool." "It is also cheap to repair," Sarah added. She had checked the grammar of their report. She explained the sensor and the program, and answered the judges' questions with a swift confidence. One judge, an intellectual with a thin face, asked whether the device could work in a true desert. "It can work anywhere there is a water source," Sarah said. "The settings are adjustable." Another judge asked about export. "We have not thought that far," David admitted. It was a brave admission. "But the design is simple enough to be copied by any skilled workshop." Mark entertained them with a joke about sheep, and even the thin-faced judge laughed. When the bell rang, David felt unable to breathe.

The award ceremony was held in the same hall. There were prizes for three teams and a distinction for the project that best served the community. David did not pray, but he held his breath. When the mayor read them out, Mark stood up so fast that his chair fell over, and Sarah covered her face. Their project won the distinction, and the glory was almost too much. Their immediate reward was a certificate; the highlight was the moment the whole hall turned to look at them. A photographer asked them to stand together, and David, still unable to believe it, held the certificate up. It was incredible. "You should be proud of yourself," Sarah said. "We succeeded." That evening they went to a pub near the museum and ordered tea and a plate of peanuts. The tea had a strange flavor, but nobody complained; Mark, who had lost his appetite, only drank half. Mark proposed a toast to the faithful friend who lent them the van, and to the kindness of strangers.

The fair taught David something he had not expected. He had assumed that science was a matter of clever ideas, but the bulk of it was patience: testing, failing, testing again. There was progress, slow and uneven. The project was a pilot, and the professor said it might help other villages in the valley. "The market for this is large," he said. "Not only here. The whole of mankind needs water." David was not sure, but he was hopeful. On the calendar, the date of the fair was circled in red, and he would remember it for a lifetime. It was a lesson worth learning, and he wondered whether next year would bring another. The ocean wind was cold. He and Sarah walked back through the empty court, and he told her he wanted to continue the project. "Then we will," she said. "But first, sleep. You look terrible." Now he laughed, and the sound came out so loud that a guard turned to look. It was the first time he had felt so light, and he carried it up the stairs, into the dormitory, and into a deep sleep.
