March came in with wind and a rumor. On the second Wednesday of the month, at 11:40 in the morning, an anonymous post appeared on the campus forum. It claimed that the team had signed a secret deal with a corporation and was selling user data for money. Anyone could download the app in a minute, it said. It included a short clip of that screen and a screenshot of a contract with a red circle around a clause. Within an hour the thread had thirteen replies, and by noon, ahead of any official response, it had been shared in every dormitory group on campus. The topic trended for two days. David read the post twice in the office upstairs and felt the floor move under him. Everybody in the team had always known that trust was their only real asset. Now a stranger with no name was telling six hundred users that the asset was fake. The thread burst into a continuous stream of notifications. Most comments were confused rather than hostile, but a few were already certain of his guilt.

Due to the rumor, thirty users decided to withdraw their accounts by the next morning. David could not grasp how a single post had done so much damage, and only later did they learn the extent of it. He was mad, then tired, then sat on the staircase with his head in his hands and could hardly breathe. Sarah brought him a carton of juice and sat beside him without speaking, and her sympathy was more useful than advice. Mark wanted to strike back at once with a long public reply, but David knew that anger would only feed the story. "Any answer will look guilty," he said. "We have to acknowledge the risk, but we also have to show the evidence." Despite the severe pressure, they agreed on a rule: no one would post anything until they had the facts. A few of the comments were rude, and Shelf suffered for three weeks, but the consequence of one careless sentence could be worse than the rumor itself.

Professor Chen, their academic advisor, listened to the whole story in his office and stroked his gray beard. He was a generous man with a slow manner. "A rumor is a fire," he said. "You cannot put it out by arguing with the flame. You remove the fuel." He asked them to underline every claim concerning the app and to mark which ones could be tested. The exercise stimulated logic and discipline. Mark made a side-by-side comparison with the real app and found that the fonts differed: the fake used a pronoun where the real interface used a noun, and the button colors were slightly wrong. A simple technique made the difference obvious. The contract in the image was an old press release from a corporation, with a clause added in a different typeface. The server logs showed no prior connection to any foreign address, and the data input page had no field for phone numbers at all. "You have an option," the professor said. "You can answer with a better story, or you can advance the truth in small, solid steps. I would offer the second. The latter is slower, but it is the only one that lasts." He had heard their pitch in December, he added.

The post had been uploaded from a computer in the art building, a circular hall twenty meters past a ditch and a low hedge. David walked there on a weekday afternoon, through a narrow passage between two old halls, and climbed the staircase to the second floor. The art studio upstairs smelled of paint, timber, and electricity. A painter was working at an easel, and a student with a pencil was sketching a horse from a photograph. Wang was there, sitting on a stool with his laptop on his knee. His application in December had been one of forty, and they had selected twelve; he had never received a letter. Wang did not look up when they entered, but his hand stopped moving. "I think you know why we are here," David said. Wang's face went white, and then red. He looked guilty, and he did not deny it. He possessed a laptop with the same hardware as Mark's, and on the table beside him lay a pair of scissors and a page of notes. "I felt invisible," he said. "You were indifferent to people like me." David had to bend down to pick up a pencil from the floor, and he placed it on the table. "That is true," he said. "We neglected that. But it does not excuse this."

That evening the department held its spring barbecue on the lawn behind the engineering building. Wang came at David's invitation and stood at the edge. The air smelled of smoke and grilled cheese. Mark handed him a carton of juice and a cup of ice. Then Wang said, quietly, "I was a coward. I wrote it because I wanted someone to blame. I told myself it was only a joke." He admitted that he had been a slave to envy, and that he had not expected the post to travel so far. "Oh god," Sarah said, "you wrecked a month of work with one click." "What do you want?" David asked. "I want to fix it." David felt no satisfaction in seeing him so small, but he wanted a decent ending. "Then write the truth," he said. "Not an excuse, not a speech. Just the truth." Wang nodded and went home early.

It was one thing to plan a correction and another to execute it. On Friday Wang delivered the correction himself: he had invented the story, the screenshot was false, and Shelf had never sold any data. The original entry was replaced with a warning, and no fact was missing from his apology. A small crowd awaited the correction outside. Trust, unlike a rumor, does not travel at the speed of a click; it moves at the speed of a conversation. They launched a quiet campaign of small meetings, one dorm at a time, where users could interact with the team. They implemented a clear privacy page, enabled two-step verification, and added a filter that flagged anonymous posts containing the word "scam". Their preference was for explanation rather than argument, and their methods were economical. A student representative from each dorm was invited to review their data policy, and the first report gave them a modest boost. Meanwhile, the bookstore restored its pilot, and the locker points reported a steady output of trades. The team chose to spend weeks trying to restore what one post had taken, and their own blog entry reached more readers than the rumor. Similarly, the volunteers who had stayed became more careful and more loyal. It was relatively little compared with a corporation's marketing budget, but it was enough.

In April the team took a weekend trip to a small island. The ferry cruised across a flat gray sea, and a flock of gulls followed the boat, their feathers bright in the wind. The island had a single road, a few farms, and a population of rabbits with soft fur that had learned to ignore tourists. It was famous for them. Public transportation was almost nonexistent, so they walked. A foreigner with a camera asked them for directions, and Mark, who had hired a horse for an hour, pointed helpfully from the saddle. He came back with a bruise on his hip; Sarah's cousin, a nurse, cleaned a cut on his leg where he had caught it on the blade of a broken fence. They barbecued fish, drank juice with ice, and recycled every carton and bottle. In the afternoon they walked to the far end of the island, past a ditch, a water pump, and a windbreak of timber, and found the wreck of an old fishing boat half buried in the grass. A low limb of a tree hung over it, and a painted sign warned that a poisonous plant grew nearby. David kneeled to read it, and for a moment the whole rumor felt very far away. A serious athlete from the rowing club ran past, and Mark, who had eaten too much cheese, watched him with a variety of feelings that were mostly envy. The afternoon was free.

At the opening ceremony of the spring term, the university asked David to speak about academic honesty. He stood at the front of the hall in a white shirt, with the whole faculty ahead of him. He did not give a speech about virtue in the abstract. He told the story of the rumor, the fake screenshot, and the thirteen replies. He said that a society is held together by small acts of accuracy, and that the soul of a university is the habit of checking before believing. "A rumor is a test," he said. "It asks what you are willing to pass on without proof." When he finished, there was a short silence, and then warm applause. Professor Chen nodded from the third row. Afterwards, Sarah said that he had looked like a representative of something larger than a startup, and Mark said that he had looked nervous. David knew that the episode would become a phase in the story of Shelf, not its end. The company was still short of money, and there would be other storms. But the team had learned the supreme lesson of the year: the truth is slower than a lie, and it lasts longer.
