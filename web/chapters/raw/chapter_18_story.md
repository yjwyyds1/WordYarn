On the first weekend of December, David applied to join the campus newspaper. The Sentinel had its office downstairs in the basement of the old library, underneath the stairs, in a room beneath a low ceiling that smelled of damp paper and hot dust. A furnace rumbled behind a door, a clock on the wall ran four minutes fast, and a crack ran from the ceiling to the rug. The editor was Lin Mei, a fourth-year student with a quiet voice and an advanced sense of deadlines. She read his sample article, asked two simple questions, and readily agreed to take him on. "We are a small paper," she said, "but we are a certain kind of small paper. We print what we can prove." The office was buried in old issues, and the noise of the press came up through the floor, a sound they had all learned to ignore. David dived into the archive while the staff prepared the Christmas special; he was awake until two that morning and did not mind. The atmosphere was noisy, warm, and serious.

His first assignment was a feature on the campus barber. Mr. Wu had cut hair in a narrow shop near the north gate for thirty years, and he was closing it in January. David found him on a Tuesday morning, sweeping hair from a rug beside a red and white pole. A small beast of a cat slept on the windowsill; Mr. Wu said it was a rare breed and the shop's true owner. "Sit," he said. "You are not here for a haircut, but sit anyway." The shop smelled of soap and warm cloth. On the wall hung a painting of the old campus, and beside it a photograph of Mr. Wu in July 1994, young and proud in his first shop. He had come to the city after the war, he said, with a knife, a brush, and a few pennies in his pocket.

He wore old boots, and the heels were worn flat. He had cut the hair of three generations. His wrist moved in a slow, steady march as he worked. "Another customer," he said, "is another acquaintance. That is the whole business." A student came in to inquire about a haircut, and Mr. Wu sewed a loose button on the boy's coat while they talked. When David asked about his wife, Mr. Wu looked at the floor. She was in hospital, he said, and the shop would close so he could care for her. The shop stood beside a nursery that had once been a bakery. He would not accept pity and did not want to be scolded for working too long. Then he charged David nothing and told him to come back before January.

Back in the office, the staff gathered around a long table to plan the Christmas special. From the outset, Lin wanted a selection of stories that would make the paper feel like a present: a concert review, a column on economics, a recipe for a simple dessert, and a striking photograph of the first snow. There was an amateur cartoonist on the staff, a quiet genius named Tao, who drew a cartoon of a small and slightly angry beast for the cover; it was funny without being cruel. The paper's rival, a student magazine, had already printed its special, but Lin was not worried. "They are dull," she said. "We are not." The staff was mostly young, and various ideas filled the table. The design editor, who was pregnant, worked from home and sent layouts by email. Sarah argued for a science page about a chemical experiment, technology, and the electronic newsletter, and Tao wanted an optional page of crossword puzzles. In the end they decided on a mix, and the interaction between words and pictures was carefully planned. Mark's only possession of value was a camera, and they put it to work. The pages had to be sewed into a mock-up by Sarah's hands, because the printer's machine could not handle the small format.

On Thursday a strange and mysterious phenomenon appeared on the campus network. The first symptom was a missing file; soon students reported that their documents were disappearing and that a message with a red envelope was circulating in their email. The office computer caught the virus too, and a flood of warnings filled the screen. David took a torch and went with Sarah to the server room under the science building. With the aid of a simple program, they isolated the infected files and traced the message to a dormitory address. The method was logical: they compared the sender's time stamp with the exam schedule and found a gap of thirty minutes. It was not a state attack or a corporate spy; it was a prank, a trap set by a student who wanted to see how the newspaper would react. There was no one to blame but the student. Consequently, the story had legal implications: he had broken a rule, but no real harm had been done. The editor decided not to name him. "We can report the phenomenon without crushing one individual," Lin said. The criticism could still sting, but David understood the implication. They published a warning and a short guide to protecting files.

The final week was a race. The printer, an external contractor, warned that a paper shortage might restrict them to its limit of twelve pages; Lin negotiated a discount and a later deadline, and they signed a short contract on the spot. The executive editor, a nervous man with a clipboard, timed every stage with his watch. They had to measure each column to the millimeter and correct every proof by hand. One photograph would not reproduce clearly, so Sarah enclosed the original negative in an envelope and hired a van to take it to the printer. "Make sure the date is stamped on every box," Lin said. "And pronounce the issue ready only when the last page is on the press." They worked within a budget that was sufficient but not generous. The result was satisfactory: forty pages, a gold masthead, and a full-color cartoon. "We did not foresee the paper shortage," Lin admitted, "but we survived it." At two in the morning she told them to go home and sleep. It was the final edition she would sign.

The special issue came out on the last Friday of term. Mark loaded the bundles onto his bicycle and made the delivery himself, because the usual van had broken down. A passenger on the campus bus read the barber's story and missed her stop; a professor laughed at Tao's cartoon on the front page; a fan of the paper wrote to say that it was the first time she had seen her own street in print. The feature had made a difference. Within a week the barber's shop received more customers than it had seen in a year, and Mr. Wu, who had planned to close quietly, agreed to stay until the spring. "A pity about the hospital," Sarah said, "but at least the shop will not die." The paper circulated more widely, and for the first time in months its small budget looked almost comfortable. At the staff meeting Lin spoke about freedom and responsibility, and then announced that she would step down in January. "The Sentinel needs a new editor," she said. "My priority now is my thesis. The paper's mission is bigger than any one of us." She looked around the room and smiled. "The selection is yours."

The farewell party was held on a Saturday evening in the office, with fruit punch, ice-cream, and a guitar that Tao played badly but with love. Someone had brought a cake, and crumbs were soon under the table. Lin stood on a chair and thanked everyone by name. "You are a strange and stubborn breed," she said. "You ask questions for a living. Do not stop." Mark raised his cup. "Yes, madam," he said, and everyone laughed. David gave her a small gift: a brass compass he had bought at the market, because, he said, she had kept the paper pointed north. Lin looked at it for a long moment, then handed him a torch. "For the basement," she said. "And for the stories that are hard to see." They talked until midnight, and for once nobody mentioned the deadline and everyone could relax. Outside, they crossed the grass of the quad. David walked home with one sock wet from the snow and did not care. He felt the year turn, slowly, upward.

On the first Monday of January, the paper elected its new editor. The choice was Sarah, and David became features editor; Mark, to no one's surprise, took charge of delivery. The office was still damp, the furnace still rumbled, and the clock still ran four minutes fast. But the first issue of the new term was on the table, and the staff read it together in the public sphere of the common room, where anyone could comment. David thought about the barber, the virus, the cartoon, and the long nights at the long table. He had learned that a newspaper is not a collection of opinions; it is a set of small promises, kept one issue at a time. The academy called it media studies, but in the basement it was a simple habit of checking, asking, and telling the truth. Whatever happened in the everyday life of the campus, the pages would be there on Friday, and someone would read them.
