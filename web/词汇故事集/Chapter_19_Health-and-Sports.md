# Chapter 19 · Health and Sports 健康与体检

> **主题**：康复、体检、慈善跑

## 正文

February brought a health check-up for every second-year student, and David went to the campus clinic on a Tuesday morning with a blank form in his hand. The waiting room was noisy, the furniture was old, and the queue moved slowly across the tiled floor. A notice on the wall listed the timetable: height, weight, blood pressure, and a blood test, each in a different room. His mother had once spent a month in a ward after a small operation, and the smell of soap and paper had stayed with him. He filled in the form and answered a short inquiry about his habits, and tried to understand the decimal numbers on the scale. A knock on the door called him inside. The doctor, a gentle woman with a microscope on her desk, looked at his results and concluded that his blood pressure was too high for a man of his age. "It is not a crisis," she said. "But it is an issue. You need exercise, less sugar, and a better timetable."

He had spent the winter at a desk that had become a prison, living on soda, soup, and whatever the canteen served. The smell of soup stirred his appetite every evening. He knew the food was harmful, but it was cheap. Contrary to what he had assumed, the problem was not his age; in his case, the cause was simple.

The doctor gave him a small magazine about healthy eating and a weekly plan. "Do not try to change everything at once," she said. "That is the most common mistake. You can start with one habit." She wrote a single sentence on the margin of his form: Move every day. On the way out he passed the chemist's counter and bought a bag of nuts instead of a chocolate bar, and he threw the trash from his bag into the bin. He did not want one bad habit to ruin his health. Outside, the air was cold and clean, and he stood for a moment on the steps, breathing. The idea of running had never interested him. He had always believed that his body was a limitation rather than an advantage, and that it demanded too much attention. He had a prejudice against sport, as if it belonged to a different kind of person. But he was twenty, and the doctor's numbers were a warning he could not ignore.

The university would host a charity run in April, ten kilometers along the river, to raise money for a rural school and for the conservation of the river and its environment. The aim was modest, but the event had a motto that made him stop: Every step is a donation. David signed up, paid the small fee, and received a timetable of training sessions. Sarah joined for the cause; Mark, for the challenge. Their coach was an older student named Zhou, a local legend in the running club, who had finished the city marathon three times. "Forget speed," he said at the first session. "We are not training for a medal. We are training for the finish line. Whoever finishes gets the same soup." He appointed David the team's timekeeper, a task that involved a stopwatch and a great deal of writing. "You will be punctual," he said. "The clock does not negotiate." His pronunciation of "kilometer" made everyone laugh, and the tension of the first day broke.

They met twice a week on the artificial track behind the gym, and on Sundays they ran a longer route along the river. In bad weather they used an indoor hall, where the echoing noise made conversation impossible. David could not run four hundred meters without stopping, and his face turned the color of the golden leaves still stuck to the fence. Mark, who had played football in the village, found it easy; Sarah, who danced twice a week, moved with an elegant rhythm that made the whole thing look simple. A cold breeze came off the gulf, and David's bare hands went numb. The wind seemed to freeze his ears. He wiped his face on his sleeve and tried not to look at the hill ahead. "Do not look at the distance," Zhou said.

"Look at the next tree." The route would vary each week, but the hill never did, and their first encounter with it discouraged everyone. A spider had built a web between two fence posts, and David watched it while he caught his breath, grateful for the pause. By the third week, the tedious part began: stretching, breathing, and repeating the same exercises until they became automatic. Zhou made them run an extra lap. The coach taught them to accelerate gently, not to jump at the start, and to keep something in reserve for the final kilometer. "The body is a system," he said. "Everything corresponds to everything else. Hurt one part, and the others compensate. That is the craft of running." David felt dumb for not having understood this sooner.

The check-up had one more stage: a blood test and a reading of his lung capacity. He went back in March and was relieved to see the numbers improve. The doctor was satisfied, though she reminded him that progress was not a straight line. "You will have bad days," she said. "Do not let one bad day ruin the whole month." She told him to reduce salt and to sleep more. She implied that he might need medicine later, but for now the exercise was enough. He began to understand his own shortcoming: he wanted results too quickly. In the waiting room he met a neighbor from his dorm, a young economist who taught at the business school and was training for the same run for a different reason. "My father had a heart attack," the neighbor said.

"The doctors say exercise is beneficial, so here I am." An older man in the corner, a retired philosopher with a small pension, listened and laughed. "I have run every day for forty years," he said. "Not because I am wise. Because I am stubborn. I first came to this city on a student visa, and I never left." The waiting room held people of every age and sex. It was a diverse group, and the conversation was the best medicine of the day. Under the circumstances, the doctor's advice was the only sensible plan. The scope of the plan was small, but it was his. In total, David had run forty kilometers that month, and the measurement on the clinic's chart had moved in the right direction. His former self would not have believed it. Running had seemed a lonely sport, but the team had made it social. He felt a new passion, and with it the possibility of a different life.

The morning of the run arrived with a clear sky and a cold wind. The run took place on the first Sunday of April. David combed his hair, put on his new pair of fancy running shoes, and took the bus downtown with Mark and Sarah. The route began at the old bridge, followed the river through the urban streets, and turned back at the edge of the forest. Police officers had closed the road, and stands of supporters lined the surface of the street, holding signs and cups of soda. A string of runners stretched ahead of them, a river of color. The destination was the same bridge, seen from the other side. When the starter gave a knock on a wooden board, the crowd let out a cheer. He had not an atom of natural speed, but he had a plan. At the third kilometer he felt the old pain in his knee.

He could not tell whether it was real or only fear, and for a moment he wanted to cry. Then he saw an old woman at the border of the crowd, holding a sign that said, "Thank you for my granddaughter's school." The sign seemed to compel him to keep going. The last kilometer was the hardest, and he had to tell himself to accelerate. Close to the finish, the crowd grew louder. He did not beat the winner, but he beat his own time by four minutes, and the release of tension at the finish line was better than any award. It amazed him that his legs could carry him so far. Finally, the bridge appeared. Mark finished ahead of him, Sarah finished beside him, and the three of them stood together in the laughter of the crowd. For a morning the street became a small republic, and the old racial divisions meant nothing on the road. It was certainly not easy, but everyone smiled.

Afterwards they ate soup and bread in a hall near the finish, and David wrote a short piece for the campus paper about the day. He was not a natural athlete; that was simply true. But he had acquired a habit, and he had learned something about himself: his body was not a prison, and health was not a mystery. It was a system of small choices, repeated until they became easy. His mother called that evening, and he told her about the run. "I am grateful," she said, "that you are taking care of yourself." He looked at the medal on the table, a small golden circle with a red string, and understood that the real prize was the habit. Whoever ran next year would have a different story, and the route would vary, but the finish line would still be there.
