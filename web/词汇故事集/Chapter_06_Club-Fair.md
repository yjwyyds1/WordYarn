# Chapter 06 · Club Fair 社团招新

> **主题**：招新、计算机社、写给奶奶的信

## 正文

The club fair opened on the first Saturday of November, and by nine the main square was full. Flags hung from every booth, the sunshine was warm for the season, and the ground was still wet from the rain of the previous night. David, Mark and Sarah walked slowly down the lane between the stalls, Mark alongside Sarah. It was apparent that the population of the campus had come: every inhabitant of the dormitories, every young student with time on his hands, and a few teachers who had come to see what the clubs had made. These were the people David saw every day. The eighth booth belonged to the drama society, which had built a small stage out of boxes. A man in a paper crown was celebrating the opening by shouting the club's name. In the confusion of music and voices, nobody knew where to go.

The computer club's table stood near the science building. The club had been founded two years earlier, and its members had invented a small machine that could sort coins by size. "The task is simple," said a serious girl in glasses. "The machine uses a dial and a rotating disc. You rotate the dial, and the disc sends each coin down a different curve." David watched the coins roll into separate boxes and felt his brain wake up. The machine was practical rather than beautiful, but it worked. "It can handle multiple tasks," the girl said. "Its maximum speed is forty coins a minute, and the arithmetic is done by this chip." She showed him the instrument panel, where a small screen defined each step. "We derived the design from an old calculator," she said. "Do you want to sign up?" David did not hesitate.

Next to them, the Model Parliament club had set up its headquarters under a blue flag. Its members comprised students from every province and county, except the far north, including two exchange students. They were holding a debate on a motion to abolish the old scholarship rules. "The present system is not fair," said one speaker. "It ignores the poorest students." Another argued that the budget could not balance if the rules changed, especially during a recession. They spoke about the global price of oil, the consumption of fuel, and the commodity market. The argument grew tense, and for a moment two members began to quarrel about the price of books. The secretary rang a small bell. "We must tolerate disagreement," she said, "for the sake of peace." In the end they agreed to cooperate on a new plan: a fair vote and a smaller ministry. The motion passed by a single vote.

A little farther on, the animal shelter had brought its own kind of chaos. A charity group was collecting money to build a new shelter, and a paper angel hung above the table. Rich and poor students stopped to donate a coin or two. There was a cage with two white rats, a poster about endangered species, and a photograph of an ox from a farm nearby. A toy monkey sat on the table beside a jar of snacks. "Do not try to steal the biscuits," said a volunteer, and everyone laughed. A boy was giving a small dog a bath in a basin, and the dog licked his hand and sprayed water over the crowd. It was an earnest performance, and the dog's behavior was excellent, though the ground around the basin grew dirty. A deaf volunteer used sign language to explain the shelter's work to a group of children. David put a note in the box. "Every coin helps," she signed.

At the eighth booth the drama society was selling books to raise money. There were old novels, a biography of a local doctor, and a pile of plays. Sarah picked up a book of fiction and read the plot aloud: it was about an imaginary kingdom, an angel who lost her wings, and a tragedy that ended in peace. "It is not a masterpiece," she said, "but it has emotion." The club's professional director, a humorous man with a gray beard, invited them to the evening performance. "We honor the old stories," he said. "Sometimes a silly plot teaches a serious lesson." A viewer standing nearby nodded. David bought the book for five yuan and promised to come. Beside the books, a potter sold clay bowls.

By noon they were hungry. Volunteers served snacks at a long table: bowls of rice, boiled eggs, and cakes sold by the kilo. David bought a piece of cake that weighed about an ounce. "There is no balance in your diet," Sarah said. "There is no need for balance on a Saturday," Mark replied. A boy went past skating between the booths, and a bicycle tyre hissed as it hit a stone. The strap of David's bag broke. A gust of wind made a balloon explode, and a small gas burner under a tea pot flared up. Nearby, a science student was showing how sugar dissolves in hot water, while another made a vacuum pump drip water into a glass. Dark clouds were gathering, and thunder rolled over the river. Sarah pulled her sweater around her shoulders, and they took shelter under the drama society's canvas roof.

The rain sent everyone crowding under the same roofs, and the fair became a test of patience. A young man tried to push past the crowd and knocked over a table of books. He did not apologize, and a girl told him he was a fool. The two began to argue. David stepped between them. "It is not worth it," he said. "We are all wet and tired." The girl took a breath. "You are right," she said. "I regret losing my temper." The young man picked up the books, and together they repaired the table. David offered his assistance, and the girl accepted it. The rain frustrated the organizers, but nobody complained for long. Then it began again. The drama director, who had watched the whole scene, said that it was a lesson in practical peace. "We can choose to shout," he said, "or we can choose to help. As a last resort, choose help. There is no substitute for patience." The next hour was critical for the fair, and David had to address the crowd himself. He took a breath and stepped onto the stage.

That evening David wrote a letter to his grandmother. He addressed the envelope carefully and began with the news of the fair. He mentioned the computer club, the drama society, and Sarah's little niece, who had spent the afternoon on her aunt's shoulders. "The child is an angel," he wrote. He described his new job at the campus cafe, his small budget, and his plan to renew his membership in the English club. His father, he wrote, would laugh at the price of the cakes. He told her about the biography of the village doctor, which he had found at the fair, and about the career of a young accountant he had met. "I am learning that every occupation has its own language," he wrote. "Even arithmetic has a grammar." He also wrote that the old doctor had been loyal to his patients for forty years. Nothing would change unless someone made it change, he added. He signed the letter, read it twice, and sealed it. Whenever he felt far from home, he wrote; it was the one habit he never wanted to lose.

The following Sunday was quieter. David leaned against the window of the living-room and watched the rain. The establishment of the fair had left a pile of paper on his desk, and he scattered the notes into the bin. A boat moved slowly down the river, and the curve of its wake shone in the gray light. He took a bath, put on a warm sweater, and ate a snack in the kitchen. Then he called his aunt, who told him his cousin had found a job. "She is young," his aunt said. "She will manage." David worried about her for a moment, and then let the worry go. The rain gradually stopped, and a thin line of sunshine appeared above the roofs. He went out for a walk along the river, and the air was clean and cool.

On Monday David went to the computer club's first meeting. The room was full, and the secretary handed him a form. He paid the fee, signed his name, and looked around at the members. They were a mixture of the practical and the dreamy: a boy who could fix any machine, a girl who wrote poetry, and an accountant who kept the club's budget in perfect order. David felt the old worry return for a moment, the fear that he was not the right person for this path. Then he remembered the fair, and the crowd, and the rain, and the way people had helped one another. The choice was not permanent, he knew. The notion that a career was a single decision had never convinced him. He had enough time, and enough curiosity. He did not want to live a life of regret, not for an ounce of comfort. The global economy might fall into another recession, the wealthy might keep their wealth, but a young man could still learn to build something useful. The club trip to the coast was scheduled for the spring, and he signed up for it at once.
