# Chapter 04 · First Month 第一个月

> **主题**：生病、理财、家教

## 正文

By the fourth week David had learned the true timetable of the dormitory. The sunrise came late now, and the queue for the toilet formed at seven sharp: eight boys and one bathroom. He brushed his teeth, washed his face, and pulled on his coat. The air was humid, and the corridor smelled of smoke. Breakfast in the canteen cost five yuan: rice, an egg, and a glass of milk. He ate alone on the first morning, but by the second week Mark had joined him at the same table. "You eat too fast," Mark said. "You will choke." David shrugged. "I have an early class." The canteen's cook, a woman with a tattoo of a fish on her wrist, served the rice with a steady hand. "Eat more," she said. "You are too thin." It reminded him of secondary school. Sleep, he had learned, was essential.

Money was the subject of the month. David's parents sent a payment on the first of every month, and he wrote the figures on a card that he kept in a box under his bed. The minimum he needed for food was sixty yuan a week; the rest went into an envelope for books. In addition to the monthly payment, he earned a little at the campus cafe on Saturdays. The owner, a thin man with a low voice, paid him in cash and coffee. "Honesty is rare," he said. David pocketed the money and walked to the grocer's, where fruit and garlic were cheap and the vegetables were plentiful. He bought a handful of tomatoes and a bag of rice, and he planned accordingly: the week's total must not exceed sixty yuan. He checked the figures twice; otherwise, a small error could become serious. Therefore he wrote every coin on the card. A few coins always remained at the bottom of the box.

The illness arrived on a Tuesday, and it was not a typical cold. Mark woke with a fever of forty degrees, an abnormal color in his face, and a pain under his ribs. "It is nothing," he said, and then he was sick into the basin. David took him to the clinic. The doctor could not diagnose the problem at once, so she sent them to a specialist at the city hospital. The specialist, a serious woman in a white coat, examined Mark's liver and ordered a blood test. A male nurse took the blood. "It is an infection," the specialist said. "Not fatal, but serious. This substance will help, and it will prevent the infection from spreading." Mark, lying on the bed, began to swear softly. "I have a basketball game on Friday," he said. "The league will not wait." Mark's resistance to rest was strong, but the scare lasted only three days. By Friday the fever had fallen, and Mark apologized for his temper. "You were a good nurse," he said.

The English assignment was due on the same Friday, and David had to defend his draft in front of the class. The essay was an argument about the value of failure. "A failure is information," he wrote. "It tells you where the weakness is." He read it aloud in the audio lab. The professor listened with her eyes closed, which was her characteristic habit. When he finished, she asked one question: "What is your evidence?" David had no answer. He had written a comprehensive list of opinions but forgotten the facts. "An argument without evidence," the professor said, "is a house without a floor." The grade was a low B, and he accepted it. The essay had consumed three evenings. That night he added an introduction, three examples, and a clause from a recent study. The trick, he found, was to write the truth plainly. The revision had a quality the draft lacked, and the following week was productive.

The library became his second home. It was an ancient building with high windows and a smell of paper and dust. The reading room was nearly vacant in the afternoons, and David liked the depth of the silence. He found a shelf of books about the region. One illustration showed the old harbor, with sailors, rope, and a navy ship at anchor; the sight of it made him think of his father. Another showed the fields where farmers once kept pigs. The librarian, a woman with a soft voice, helped him find information for his essay. "You have a good memory," she said. "Use it." He copied a passage onto a card and pinned it above his desk, beside a bundle of letters tied with ribbon. He found a quiet spot by the window. The library, he decided, was the only place on campus where time moved at the right speed.

In October David found a second job. A landlord in the old quarter needed someone to tutor his son, a boy of twelve who was failing English. The payment was small but steady. On the first evening David met the kid, a quiet boy named Xiao Han, and his mother, who offered tea and a plate of fruit. The father, the landlord, produced a contract with a single clause about the hours. "I do not want him to fail," he said. "I want him to learn." David explained his method: twenty minutes of reading, twenty minutes of conversation, and one short video at the end. The boy, who was fond of football, agreed to try. On behalf of the family, the mother thanked him. The job provided more than money; it gave David a reason to leave his own worries at the door. The commitment was two evenings a week, and he wrote it in his notebook. The father had enlarged a family photograph for the wall.

Mark's team played in the city league, and on Saturday David went to watch. The court was outdoors, beside a low wall, and the wind kept changing the flight of the ball. Basketball was popular that autumn. Mark's team was losing by ten points at half time. In the second half the coach called a drill that the team had practiced a hundred times. Mark stole the ball and prepared to throw it to a teammate. The throw was perfect. The teammate scored, and the crowd rose. In the final minute Mark scored the winning point. "That is the power of a drill," the coach said. "You do it until it becomes a habit." The club's voluntary helpers sold tea by the gate.

On Sunday the rain ceased, and David took the bus into the city to buy supplies. The streets were still wet, and mud covered the steps of the market. He boarded the bus with his card and stood near the door, one hand on the rail. At the market he bought a bag of rice, a box of tea, and a small jewel of a teapot for his mother. The grocer, an old man with a kind face, liked to throw in a handful of garlic for free. "For the student," he said. David stopped at a cafe for coffee and a plate of chips, and read a newspaper with a headline about the worldwide price of oil. The article said that a billion people would feel the change. He thought about his parents' payment, the minimum in his envelope, and his own small money. In a remote corner of the market, an old woman sold ribbons by the meter. The road home climbed a steep incline, and the wheel of the bus turned slowly through the humid afternoon.

On Monday morning the dormitory held a fire drill. The alarm rang at seven, and sixty boys stumbled into the courtyard in their coats. The director of the building, a retired navy man, stood on a box and instructed them to walk, not run, to the assembly point. "A fire is not a game," he said. "It is a crisis." The drill lasted twenty minutes. Afterwards David noticed a notice on the board: the building would be inspected next week, and any room that failed the inspection would lose its electricity. The director did not permit excuses. Mark cursed, because their room was a mess. They spent the afternoon cleaning: they disposed of old papers, taped a loose cable to the wall, and swept the floor. The room looked almost vacant when they finished. "It is strange," Mark said. "I did not know we had a floor."

By the end of the month David understood something that no lecture had taught him. The first month had been a series of small tests: money, illness, deadlines, and the quiet argument between homesickness and hope. He had passed some and failed others, and the failures had taught him more. He had to confront his own fear of failing. He thought of his parents in the country, of his mother's traditional baking, and of the way his father would yield the last piece of fruit to him at dinner. His uncle had emigrated to the south years ago, and the family still spoke of him at every occasion. David was not alone; Mark was asleep two meters away, and the plan for the next essay was hers. He had recently begun to sleep well. Moonlight came through the window in a tender line. The dominant feeling was not fear but gratitude. The universe was large and strange, but the room was warm. "It does not matter," he whispered to himself. "This is where I am." He turned off the light and listened to the rain.
