# Chapter 05 · Downtown 逛市区

> **主题**：老城、港口、婚礼

## 正文

The last Saturday of August fell at the end of the summer vacation, and Sarah proposed a day of sightseeing in the old town. David had never been downtown, so he agreed at once. They met at the campus gate at nine, bought tickets, and climbed aboard the bus. The traffic was heavy, and the bus moved slowly along streets crowded with tourists who seemed to invade the city every weekend. The city had spread into the countryside, and the bus passed new apartment blocks before the streets narrowed. Mark killed time by naming the shops they passed: a bakery, a shoe shop, a mall with an enormous glass roof. Near the river the old buildings leaned toward each other as if they were talking. David, a stranger to downtown, followed Sarah. "When we get there," she said, "you will see the real city." He looked out of the open window and said nothing.

Breakfast was the first business of the day. The bakery on the corner was small but excellent, and the smell of bread reached them from the doorway. The baker, a round woman with flour on her hands, sold them a warm loaf, a jar of honey, and two bars of chocolate. "Everything here is fresh," she said. "That is the only ingredient that matters." They ate at a cafeteria across the street, where the ceiling was low and the tables were crowded. Mark ordered steak with rice and a bowl of peas; David chose soup and a mug of tea. "You should drink it while it is hot," the waiter said. The food was substantial and the quantity generous. The gross weight of the loaf was nearly a kilo. David watched the bubbles in his tea and decided the meal was worth every yuan. The charge was small, and the consumer beside them, an old man with a newspaper, agreed. "Plenty of food, plenty of talk," he said. "That is a good morning." The difference between this cafe and the campus canteen was noticeable.

After breakfast they walked down to the harbor. The wind was stronger there, and the water slapped against the stone. They went aboard an old fishing boat that now carried sightseeing groups; the owner showed them a coil of rope and explained that each fiber had to be checked before the boat went out. "A rope is only as strong as its weakest fiber," he said. David looked over the side at the view of the harbor and saw a crab moving sideways on the bottom, its claw raised like a small hand. A shell lay on the pier. Gulls and pigeons fought over a piece of bread, and a fisherman baited a hook with a worm. The landscape was rough and beautiful: gray water, black rocks, and distant islands. For a moment David glimpsed the city as it must have looked a century ago. An occasional bat flew out from under the roof of a shed, though it was still day.

At noon they went to a small church for the wedding of Sarah's cousin. The building was old and cool inside, with a painted ceiling and a heavy curtain behind the altar. The minister, a kind man with a quiet humor, welcomed the guests and asked them to sign the register. Eighteen guests came, including David and Mark, invited at the last moment. The bride wore a white dress and carried a single rose; the groom looked as if he might faint. The minister spoke about patience and apology, and about how marriage binds two families. "A spouse is not a servant," he said, "but a partner." The bride's mother began to cry and pressed a handkerchief to her eyes. Sarah herself wiped her eyes and pretended she was not crying. After the ceremony the reception moved to a garden behind the church, where a saint's statue stood among the flowers. The previous week had been rainy; this afternoon was perfect.

On the way to the market they passed a jewelry shop, then a narrow lane of clothing stalls. Sarah stopped to look at a scarf, and David bought a small silver ring for his mother. The shopkeeper explained the difference between real gold and false gold, and let them examine the jewelry. The scarf was soft and smooth, and its size was right, but the price was not. "I can lower it," the woman said, "provided you buy two." Sarah bought one and David another; the woman removed the tags and wrapped them. The garment was trimmed with a thin line of silver thread, and its image in the mirror pleased Sarah. David noticed a leak in the ceiling and a line of dirt on the wall. "The owner should fix that," he said. The woman shrugged. "The owner lives in another city," she said. "Ownership is easy from far away." A license hung framed on the wall beside an old photograph.

At the end of the lane stood a small office where an old typist worked at a desk. A sign said she had been established there for forty years and could type letters for anyone. "It is a dying profession," she said, "but I am too old to specialize in anything else." A retired headmaster was waiting for a letter to his former students, and a young volunteer from the university was assisting him with it. The headmaster liked to boast that he had taught three ministers and one deputy. A committee had put up a notice about the popularity of the old market, and a powerful businessman had promised money for the roofs. David asked the typist how she earned a living now. "Carefully," she said. "And I take care of the words." Her explanation was simple, and David liked her for it.

In the afternoon a small dispute broke out in the market. A tourist claimed that he had been charged twice for a bowl of noodles, and the shopkeeper denied it. A crowd gathered. The deputy from the local office arrived and asked both men to explain. The tourist's defense was weak; he had no receipt. The shopkeeper's account was clear, and she had a witness. The deputy listened, asked a few questions, and resolved the matter in ten minutes. The tourist apologized, and the shopkeeper accepted his apology with good tolerance. "It is a misunderstanding," she said. "It happens." There was a faint smell of smoke in the air, and for a moment the street was deadly quiet. David thought about how quickly a small thing could become serious. "You ought to write about this," Sarah said. "It is a good story." He made a mental note and wrote two lines in his notebook.

Before dinner they visited a small science exhibition in the old library. A student from the university demonstrated an excellent experiment: he connected a coil of wire to a battery, and the coil began to emit a low hum. "We compress the air here," he said, pointing to a diagram on the wall, "and this thermometer measures the change." A monitor beside the table showed the temperature in red numbers. The explanation was clear, and the instructions were printed in large letters. David, who had always liked machines, asked how the coil was made. The student showed him a cross-section of the wire. The crowd behaved well, and several children asked questions. "The whole thing is simpler than it looks," the student said. David wrote down the main points and thought that the afternoon had been more fruitful than any lecture.

They rested in a park near the harbor, where the grass was cool and the wind carried the smell of salt. Two boys were playing tennis on a cracked court, and a third was shooting arrows at a target tied to a tree. One boy had a bandage on his thumb and sweat on his face. An old gardener was trimming the hedges with slow, steady movements, and the sunset began to dominate the sky. The light turned from gold to rose to a deep, gradual blue. A critic might have called the park small, but David did not care; it was rarely so full. It was a nice evening. David lay back on the grass and looked at the clouds. "This is the best part of the day," he said. "The city is strange and noisy, but this is peaceful." Sarah nodded. "You are becoming a philosopher," she said.

Afterwards they took the bus home through the evening traffic. David's account of the day filled three pages of his notebook, and he added a few additional details on the back: the taste of the honey, the color of the bride's rose, the sound of the coil. He thought about the people they had met and the strange mixture of wealth and need in the old town. He resolved to return sometime before the end of the term. "You ought to come with us next time," he told Mark, who had fallen asleep against the window. David did not wake him. He was mature enough to know that some days could not be explained, only remembered. The future was uncertain, and problems would arise, but he would persist. He did not believe in fate, but he believed in attention. He had made a secret promise to himself: he would not let the city confuse him or frighten him. He would learn its language, and the learning would be fruitful. The bus turned forward onto the main road, and the lights of the harbor faded behind them.
