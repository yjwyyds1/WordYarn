# Chapter 11 · The Lab 实验室

> **主题**：暑期科研、神秘错误、实地调研

## 正文

Summer arrived and the campus emptied. Mark went home to help with the harvest; Sarah left for a museum internship; David stayed. Mark had been absent from the dormitory for a week before that, and the room felt strange without him. Professor Chen, who had been teaching the science workshop, had offered him a place on a small research team, and he had accepted before he understood the work. The pay was small, the laboratory was hot, and the routine began at seven. The bus took ninety minutes and stopped at a crossing near the lab, and David learned to sleep standing up. He had to accustom himself to the silence and to the long commute. By the end of the first week he was waking before the bell of his alarm. He did not know what would happen. He kept a diary in a small notebook, and on the first page he wrote: Do not pretend to understand. Ask.

The project was a survey of the bay. A new sea wall was being built near the old fort, which the council called a progressive project. The fort was a grand stone circle on the headland, built when the coast was the edge of an empire; the museum inside still kept a king's portrait, three rifles, a box of bullets, and a bomb that had never exploded. The fort had once served military intelligence. The guard showed them around and told them about the violence of the old wars. "The fort was never taken," he said. "The sea took more than any army." A spider's web hung in the corner, and a map was pinned to the wall. David admired the guard's calm. A scholar had written a long article about the fort, and the professor gave David the journal. The museum held the copyright of the photographs, used by courtesy of the director. Their assumption was simple: the sea was rising, and the wall would alter the waves.

The equipment was a mixture of the old and the new. The team had a good camera, a laptop, and an up-to-date program for recording the tide, but the sensor that measured salt was cheap and primitive. Its wire was thin, and the connection to the laptop was held together with tape. The laboratory was a converted boathouse, and its lease would expire in September. On the second day the readings began to jump. One minute the water was normal; the next it was as salty as the open sea. David checked the settings, replaced the battery, and cleaned the contacts, but the error remained. "It is invisible," he said. "I cannot see anything wrong." The professor was not worried. "Then we must make it visible," she said. "Expose the wire to the lamp and take it apart."

Life in the lab settled into a pattern. Breakfast was bread and a banana; lunch was a packet of biscuits and a pear; supper was rice and whatever the canteen had left. David kept a glove in his pocket and old trousers for the field. One afternoon a cute fox appeared on the lawn, thin and quick, and stole a biscuit from the table. It would flee when David stood up, but it came back the next day. It was fun to watch. A minor injury to his ankle gave him a reason to sit with ice and a dose of medicine. The street outside the lab was loud until midnight. Mark called from the farm and said the harvest was good, which was a favorable sign. "Are you eating?" he asked. "A banana and a biscuit," David said. "That is not a meal." "It is a necessity," David said. Mark laughed.

The first field trip took them along the highway to a village on the eastern bay. The professor drove an ancient automobile that smelled of salt and old carpet, and the porter helped them arrange and lift the gear into the back. After a long delay, they reached the village at noon. The local council had kept a census since the last century, and an agent showed them the records. An elder, the uncle of a student and an old comrade from the war, invited them in and gave them tea and a lump of sugar. He spoke in his quiet manner about how the tide had changed, and how a young sailor had once tried to swim across the bay and drowned. "The sea is not cruel," he said. "It is merely strong." He said a short prayer, and David recorded the interview in his diary. The village had no entertainment, but that night the moon was spectacular, and the silence was complete.

Back in the lab, the raw data made no sense. The sensor said the water was fresh, while the samples they had collected by hand were salty. One particular reading was impossible. The team began to debate the cause. One student rejected the sensor entirely; another assumed the samples had been contaminated. The problem seemed to stem from the instrument, but nobody could prove it. The first results were hopeless. The professor listened and said nothing. "Do not fall in love with your assumption," she said at last. "Either the instrument is wrong, or our method is." They tested the same water three times and got three different numbers, which was a shock. The outcome was clear: they could not distinguish a real change in the bay from an error in their own equipment. "The numbers are worthless," the student said. "No," the professor said. "They are telling us something. We are just not listening."

David took the sensor apart. Under the tape, the wire was cracked, and salt water had soaked into the copper. The damage was small and almost invisible, but it explained everything: when the tide came in, the water touched the crack and changed the signal. It was a skillful repair: he cleaned the wire, replaced the damaged section, and sealed it with a drop of glue. "Help me hold it," the professor said. The next readings were stable. The error disappeared immediately, and the team's mood changed at once. They could not wait. "You found it," the professor said. "How?" "I stopped trusting the tape," David said. She laughed, which she did rarely. They worked late, and by ten o'clock they had a complete record of the tide. The result was not spectacular — the sea wall would change the shore, as they had expected — but it was solid, and for the first time the project felt like his.

The town council invited the team to present their findings at a public meeting in the old fort. The room was full: fishermen, officials, a journalist, and a group of students. David spoke for twelve minutes. The news excited the village. He used no long words, and he showed one photograph after another, click by click. "The wall will protect the road," he said, "but it will also push the water toward the beach. The wall will transform the beach. It will not disappear, but it will move." There was a short silence, and then the audience began to applaud. Even the fishermen stood. A fisherman offered his opinion: the team had done in one summer what the council had failed to do in fifty years, a terrific achievement. The response was a long round of applause. The professor gave David a quick nod. The team deserved credit, and he felt a shock of pride. They left in a hurry before the questions began.

Afterwards there was supper in the museum courtyard. The council provided bread, cheese, and a large pot of fish soup; someone brought a guitar, and the music drifted over the wall toward the bay. The soup was hot on David's tongue. He sat with the elder's nephew and a young woman, and they talked about the fort, the fishing, and the price of fuel. "You are lucky," the woman said. "You get to leave." "I am not sure that is luck," David said. "It is merely a different life." He could hear the tide coming in, soft and steady. The moon rose over the water. David thought about the long summer, the hot lab, the cracked wire, and the old man who said the sea was merely strong. He had learned more in ten weeks than in a year of lectures, and he knew that he would remember the night for a long time.

The professor asked him to stay for the autumn term and help write the report. The work continued into October. The report was published in a small journal before Christmas, and the team received a grant to continue the survey. David was listed as a co-author, an honor for a student. The success was largely due to the professor, who liked to teach by asking questions rather than giving answers. The project had settled into his mind as a new perspective on science: it was not certainty but a way of asking questions, and it depended on people willing to admit they had been wrong. That was the most valuable thing he had learned. It was no miracle, only patience; indeed, it was the opposite. Soon the campus was full again, and Mark was back from the farm with a bag of pears. "You look different," Mark said. "You look like a scholar." "I came here as a youth," David said, "and now I am content to be a student." Mark laughed. "Nowadays that is the same thing," he said.
