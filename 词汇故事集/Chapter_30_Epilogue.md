# Chapter 30 · Epilogue 周年与终章

> **主题**：回访母校、周年聚会、尾声

## 正文

A year passed more quickly than David had expected. On the first Saturday of June he took the train back to Haizhou, via the coast road, with a fortnight off and a bag that was lighter than the one he had carried out of the dormitory. He had actually been planning the trip for months, though he usually left such things to chance. Mark had organized the reunion, and Sarah had booked a table at the old restaurant. The train moved through the green fields, and David watched the sea appear and disappear beyond the window. He had accomplished less in his first year than he had hoped, and more than he had feared. That, he suspected, was the ordinary shape of a life.

The campus had changed. A tall new building stood on the site of the old gym, designed by a former classmate who was now an architect, and the fence in front of it was covered with drawings. They had begun to construct a new library behind the hall. The old residence had been repainted, and the graduation assembly hall was full of light. A small museum showed photographs of every class, and David found his own diploma in a glass case, next to a letter from his first interview. The paths that had once been separate were now joined by a bridge, and some of them had been repaved. The whole place looked more attractive than he remembered. "The improvement is remarkable," he said. "It is not unique," Mark said. "Every university does this." A young engineer from the alumni office gave them a map and a badge, and David felt, for a moment, like a visitor in his own native place.

They met Sarah in the courtyard outside the library. She had cut her hair again and wore a fashionable green coat, and she hugged them both with an affection that made David's throat tight. The conversation was loud and fast, the way it had always been. She worked for an architecture firm now, but she still painted on Sundays, and she had brought a drawing of the pond to show them. "I am trying to compose a series," she said. "A story in pictures, one paragraph at a time." Mark said that he preferred jazz to her classical music, and she said that jazz was only classical music in a hurry. They argued about literature, about films, and about who had been the worst singer in the dormitory. It was the same argument they had been having for five years, and none of them wanted it to end.

The reunion picnic was held on the grass beside the pond. The afternoon was pleasant, with a warm sun and a light wind, and the cherry trees along the bank were still in blossom. A deer stood at the edge of the trees. Sarah had brought a basket with bread, fish, cabbage and a cold omelette, which Mark called protein; David had brought a kettle of tea. A gardener was sweeping the path with a broom, and the moist earth smelled of fertilizer. They put the basket under the tree and spread a cloth, and folded their jackets under their heads, and watched the yellow flowers. A curl of smoke rose from a barbecue on the other side of the pond. Their bicycles lay on the grass, and the pattern of the branches moved across the water. After lunch they walked around the lake. For an hour nobody talked about work.

In the afternoon they rode their bicycles to the seaside. The road was bound for the old harbor, and it twisted along the cliff above the water. Mark tried to overtake a bus and had to halt at the next bend, laughing. He lowered the saddle and checked the tires. The sea was a deep channel of blue, and beyond it an extinct volcano rose from the water like a sleeping animal. "They say you can see a whale from here in spring," Sarah said. "Not today," David said. The wind was cool, with a chill that made them zip their jackets. They left the bicycles in a garage by the beach and walked down to the coarse sand, where the ivy began to creep over the wall and the light began to soften. Far out, a fishing boat traced a white line across the water.

On Sunday they drove to the village where Mark had grown up. His family kept a small herd of goats, and his father still worked at the old factory that made parts for bicycles. The factory had once exported to half the world, but the trend had turned, and now it employed a few hundred workers instead of a few thousand. "The dollar goes further than it did," Mark's father said, "but everything is more expensive." He showed them a receipt from 1990, when a pound of meat had cost almost nothing, and a newspaper page of statistics about inflation. Nobody agreed on the cause. The village had a new road, a new school, and a new level of commerce: a shop that sold tobacco, tea, and mobile phones. A sign said that the governor had visited in April. "He came with a man from the embassy," Mark's father said. "They promised a million yuan for the factory. We are still waiting."

On Monday the three of them visited the old laboratory, which had been turned into a small museum. The first computer they had used still stood in the corner, a heavy beige device with a keyboard like a typewriter. A lamp was suspended above it. A guide explained the theory behind the sensor project, and David found that he could still analyse the old data in his head. "The process was simple," she said. "The efficiency was terrible, but the idea was a revolution." A screen showed a scan of the original drawing, and beside it a photograph of the machine that had produced the first spark of the project. "There was an error in the maintenance log," David said, "and a student smashed a bottle in the basement." "That was you," Mark said. "It was not," David said. "It was the ghost." They laughed, and the sound hung in the quiet room.

That evening the class gathered at the old restaurant. The volleyball captain came with his wife and a small girl who hid behind his leg, shy among so many strangers. The former class leader, now a confident young manager, stood up and said that the reunion had a sponsor: a local company that had agreed to pay for the dinner. "A match made in heaven," someone said. The old restaurant was dark and romantic. They talked about Professor Chen, who was ill and could not come, and for a moment there was real grief in the room. "Nevertheless," the captain said, "she would want us to eat." The wine was cheap and the jokes were old, and David felt the old thrill of being part of something. He was tempted to make a speech, but he resisted the temptation and raised his glass instead. "To the next year," he said. "And the one after."

Later, the three of them sat on the steps outside. "So," Sarah said, "are you happy?" David thought about it. "Basically, yes," he said. "The work is hard, and the pressure is real, but it is not a bad life." Mark said that he had felt the same at first, though he had wanted to quit in January. "The worst obstacle was myself," he said. "I kept thinking I was stupid." "You are not stupid," Sarah said. "You are merely lazy." "I beg to differ," Mark said. "Do not dismiss the random," she added. "We are all dependent on something." They talked about the future, about whether to stay in the city or go home, and about the random chances that had brought them together. "I refuse to believe it was all random," Sarah said. "Perhaps it was not," David said. "However, I would not exaggerate the design." "Anyway," Mark said, "I am glad we came." "Instead of what?" Sarah asked. "Instead of sitting at home," he said, "talking to oneself."

David stayed one more night in the old town. He woke before dawn and walked to the harbor, where the fishing boats were already moving out and the sun was rising behind the hills. The frost of the early morning had begun to melt on the roofs. He thought about the year behind him and the people who had helped him. He had not accomplished anything remarkable, he knew; he had simply kept going, and that was its own kind of achievement. On the train home, he wrote a short letter to Professor Chen, and then he looked out of the window at the sea. Each place leaves a trace, and each person leaves a story. He was not sure where his own story would end, but he was no longer afraid of the way it might go.
