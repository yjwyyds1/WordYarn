# Chapter 28 · Graduation 毕业

## 正文

April came, and with it the exact date of the graduation. The university hung a banner across the main gate, and the campus began to talk of nothing else. It was a yearly affair, but for David it felt like the first one ever held. His parents would come down from the village, and Mark's family would drive over from the farm. The two of them developed a plan on the back of an envelope: who would meet which parent and where they would eat. The expectation in the dormitory was mixed with panic. "Two months," Mark said. "Two months, and we are nobody again." David did not answer. He was thinking about the date on the envelope, and about how quickly it had arrived.

The cleaning of the dorm began early. In the basement they found the debris of four years: broken chairs, a couch with one leg missing, a box of disks that no one could read, and a jar in which something green had once been an organism. "Do not open that," Sarah said. Out of curiosity, and never lazy, Mark opened it anyway. The smell was enough to make a dog leave the room. Sorting four years into boxes was a complicated business, and every object fell into a category: keep, store, throw away. David helped draw a poster for the wall, and Mark, who was ignorant of art, added a giant smile to it. At the bottom of a box they found a computer mouse, its last user unknown, and a formula sheet from their first physics class, which made them both quiet for a moment. The chairs upstairs were inferior, Mark said, but the basement had more history. "We were children," he added. "No," David said, "we were students. There is a difference." The old gang came and went all afternoon.

In May, Sarah played the lead in a student play at the little theater beside the library. She was a natural actress, and the role required both patience and a sharp sense of timing. The piano was played live, and her performance was the most fascinating part of the evening. David sat in the third row and observed her. Between the acts, the drama coach, a keen woman in a red scarf, interrupted the applause to clarify a line that the audience had misheard. "She is sensitive to every word," David whispered. "That is the talent," Mark said. After the show, Sarah confessed that she had nearly forgotten her opening line. "I could feel the whole hall looking at me," she said. "A role can imprison you if you let it. But the stage is a good place to study people." It was, David thought, the most honest sentence he had heard all year.

Graduation day was hot and bright. The ceremony was held in the great hall, and the whole event was broadcast live on the university channel. The president spoke first, in a dark uniform that made him look like a commander, and then a guest speaker took the stage: a widow who had spent thirty years working with refugees. Volunteers greeted the families at the door. She was a survivor of a war herself, and she spoke without notes. "Education is the one thing nobody can take from you," she said. "It is the seal on a promise you make to yourself." The national anthem was sung by a student choir, and the audience stood. The procedure was simple: names were read, students crossed the stage, and the president shook each hand. Afterwards there was a modest reception with tea and cakes, and an old lady from the housing office pressed a paper flower into David's hand. "You will do well," she said. It was a convention, of course, but it felt like a blessing.

David's parents arrived the night before, and the university had set aside accommodation for families in the northern dormitory. It was clean, and his mother said it was better than the hotel they had priced in town. Mark's parents came too, with his small sister and a nephew who was still a child. They reserved a table at a restaurant near the station, and the eight of them ate dinner together. The dishes were simple: fish, potato, greens, and a liter of tea. Sarah brought a jar of her mother's sauce and put it in the middle of the table. The bill was reasonable, and David's father paid it before anyone could argue. "This is a good day," he said. His voice was quiet, but everyone heard him.

On the morning of the ceremony, the campus was crowded with families and cameras. The library and the gardens attracted a steady stream of visitors; the crowd was mainly parents and grandparents. David and Mark walked to the hall, a route they had walked twice a day before. "This is the exact spot where I fell off my bicycle," Mark said. "That is a fact," David said. "I was there." They passed the bench that had been their first meeting place. Beyond the gate the city was changing, and the university was changing with it; a new tower was rising behind the gym, and the whole district felt dynamic. In an era of construction, one aspect of the campus remained the same: the old stone gate. "For instance, that tower will be finished soon," Mark said. "No two of us were alike, and the span of four years had changed us more than the buildings," David said. By the time they reached the hall, the choir was already singing. Then they laughed, because it was not true, and because it was.

The weather had been rainy for three days, and the forecast threatened a storm overnight. A massive tent had been set up on the field, but its capacity was smaller than the crowd, and the university hesitated. The decision was governed by a simple rule: if the rain continued past seven, the ceremony would move inside. At eight sharp, the rain stopped, but the ground was wet. The officer in charge wanted to protect the grass, and anyone could see the effect of the rain on the field. David saw a girl slip and cut her knee, a bloody scratch that she laughed off. "It will spoil the photographs," someone said. The solution was to lay a long wooden floor across the worst of the mud. "This is a firm plan," the officer said. His voice was calm and neutral, and it made people calmer too. By nine, half the field was dry.

That night the class held a party in the common room. Someone had hung paper lanterns, and someone else had brought a speaker that was too loud. Mark told a story about the basement that amused everyone, and Sarah was soon engaged in a conversation about the future. The review of her play in the campus paper had been kind. David could not focus on the jokes. The old gang sat on the floor in a circle, and the talk turned to the future. "I have a confession," Mark said. "I am not ready." Nobody laughed. Sarah said that leaving was a kind of sacrifice, and that the loss would come later, in small pieces. "We will email," she said. "We will not," Mark said. David took out the small notebook in which he had kept a vocabulary of the four years. He read a few words aloud, and the room grew quiet. "A deed is what remains after the words," he said. "That is not a saying," Sarah said. "It is your own." He shrugged. "Then it is a good one." Four years, he thought, constitute a large part of a life, and nobody wanted to leave first.

Two days before the departure, the three of them took a coach to a small town in the western hills. They had found it by chance in their first year. The town had an old mill by the stream, a field of hay, and a wooden fence that leaned with age. A frog, a small creature with a loud voice, sat in the hollow of a tree and watched them pass. The mill had once polluted the stream, but the water was clear now, and a fishing vessel from the coast was tied at the little dock. "This is the northernmost point I have ever been," Sarah said. "It is not north," Mark said. "It is west." They walked to the top of the hill and looked back at the town. "We should meet here again," David said. "Once a year." "Twice," Mark said. "If we can."

The station was full of families and bags. David's parents had taken the early coach home, and Mark's family had left the night before, so the three of them stood on the platform with their luggage and nothing to say. Sarah was going north for a temporary job, Mark east, and David to the small firm in Haizhou. "We will not cry," Sarah said, and then her voice began to tremble. Mark put an arm around her. "It is only a year," he said. The train arrived, and they embraced, one by one. David waved until the carriage was out of sight, and then watched the people who remained. He would pursue his own work in the city, and the summer would pass, and the yearly reunion would come. Beyond the station roof, the sky was clearing. He did not look backward. He picked up his bag and walked toward the exit.
