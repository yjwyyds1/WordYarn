# Chapter 25 · Love and Growth 爱与成长

> **主题**：告白、恋爱、奶奶生病

## 正文

By April David could no longer deny it: he was in love with Sarah. The feeling had grown quietly, and now it filled his attention. Mark noticed before he said a word. "You are not just friends," Mark said one evening. "It is obvious." David told him to be quiet. "Love is blind," Mark said, "but the rest of us can see." David had been single all year; he had told himself that study came first, and that love was a barrier he could not afford. But the barrier had become a door. He hated the waiting, the way he rehearsed sentences in his head and then said nothing. "It is advisable to be honest," Mark said. "The worst that can happen is a no." David was not so sure.

On Saturday the English club held a picnic by the river. The basket consisted of turkey sandwiches, bread to chew, a bag of candy, and a gallon of lemonade in plastic cups. They laid a blanket on the grass; its fabric was old and soft. The day was warm, and bees moved among the purple flowers. A butterfly crossed the path, and a greedy goose came close, hoping for bread. A campus cat watched from the grass. Sarah shooed the goose away. The bank was slippery, and the path was narrow, so David offered his hand at the steep part. He had to stoop to pick up a fallen cup. They found a space on the old dock and watched an idle boat pull at its rope. He skipped a rock across the water. A mile away, a train crossed the railroad bridge, and the sound reached them a moment later. "I could stay here all afternoon," Sarah said.

David had a plan, and he had spent too long on it. He would propose a walk after the picnic, then show her the album he had made: photographs from the museum, the market, the lantern fair, the first day of class. He wanted to impress her with a gesture, but every version sounded formal and false. She had applied to an international program in design, and he wanted to better his English before he applied too. "You are overthinking," Mark said. "Say it simply." In spite of his nerves, David knew the probable answer might be no, and the thought left him helpless. He had studied the history of civilization, but not the history of his own heart. He watched Sarah talk with a friend from the club, fluent and easy, and felt the distance between them. He was a diligent student, but love was not an exam he could pass by working harder.

That evening the club ran a murder-mystery game in the old hall. Everyone wore a costume and played a character: a politician, a soldier, an evil lord, a detective, and an enemy from the past. Sarah was the detective, in a long overcoat and a hat; David was a nervous tailor with a secret. The story was a version of an old controversy: a missing painting, a false confession, suspects to eliminate one by one. Mark, disguised as a postman, was the murderer. His motive was money, and his temper gave him away. The murderer went to jail, and the room clapped. Aside from the missing screw in the frame, the room offered few clues. Sarah conducted the questioning with brilliant patience, and David noticed the screw's empty hole. "You are a model detective," she said, and patted his shoulder. The panel of senior judges gave their team the prize, and the editorial in the student paper called the game a challenge worth repeating.

On Sunday they walked to the dock again. The evening was clear, and the water held the sky. David gave her the album. She turned the pages slowly, and he watched her finger trace the photographs. "These are ordinary days," he said. "But they are the days I want to remember." She looked up. He had planned a speech, but what came out was simple. "I really like you, Sarah. Not just as a friend. I have for a long time." It would be a shame to wait any longer, he thought. The silence lasted three seconds, and it felt endless. Then she smiled, and her smile was marvelous. "I was wondering when you would say it," she said. They both laughed with relief and joy. They stood holding hands on the dock, and the world seemed wider than before. She said he had been her inspiration all year. It was unexpected happiness, the kind that arrives without a formal invitation.

Two days later the phone rang at midnight. It was his mother, and her voice was careful. It began with an accident: his grandmother had fallen, and the medical staff found that a germ had infected her lungs. The physician was worried about her heart. "She is old," his mother said, "and she is tired." David felt the room tilt. He booked a return ticket on the first train and began to pack. Mark drove him to the station on his motorcycle, and they dashed through the empty streets. The station was a rush of light and noise, and hail beat on the roof. "Send me the news," Mark said. "I will." On the train David could not sleep. The journey was a struggle; the railroad carried him south through the dark, and every mile felt like an obligation he could not meet. He thought about his grandmother's household, her small kitchen, her religious faith, the way she said a prayer before every meal.

At the hospital, the physician explained the treatment: antibiotics, rest, and time. His grandmother lay in a narrow bed, her hands folded on the sheet. The nurse checked her vital signs. "You came," she said, and her voice was weak but clear. "Of course I came." He sat beside her and held her hand. His presence seemed to comfort her. Her first complaint was a pain in her chest, but the fever had begun to drain away. She whispered a prayer to the Lord. The ward was full of ordinary sounds: a baby crying, a friendly voice, the labor of the staff. His mother brought a liquid meal, but his grandmother would not eat. "You will starve," David said, and she laughed, then coughed. He fixed a loose board with a screw and a piece of wood. He patted her hand and told her about the museum, the children, and Sarah. When he mentioned Sarah, his grandmother's eyes brightened. "Bring her next time," she said. He promised.

David helped around the house: he decorated the ward with flowers and dusted the shelves. His grandmother's condition improved, and the physician said she could return home soon. Aside from the fare, the cost was small; the welfare office covered a percentage, and the sum was smaller than he feared. The savings he had put aside for the summer were spent, but he did not regret it. "You are a good grandson," his mother said, and the words were better than any prize. He wrote to Sarah every few days; the mail was slow, but her letters were full of small news. She said she wanted to better her Chinese. David returned to his research on the bronze needle whenever he had an hour. She sent a photograph of the museum garden and a note: "The bees miss you." He laughed, and for the first time in a week he felt less tired.

When he returned to campus, the summer was beginning. Sarah met him at the station with two cups of tea. "How is she?" she asked. "Better," he said. "She wants to meet you." Sarah smiled, and the smile was answer enough. Her parents, she said, already approved. They walked toward the river. The city had changed in his absence: the surroundings were cleaner, a new garden had been planted along the bank, and a farmer's market had expanded into the square. The city had begun a reform of the riverfront, and workers had equipped the garden with benches. Sarah had helped create the garden, and she was proud of it. They stopped at the dock, where a goose was building a nest in the reeds. "It is strange," David said. "A month ago I was afraid to speak. Now everything feels possible." Sarah took his hand. "That is what happens," she said, "when you stop hiding." They stood there in the wide sunlight, and David thought that love was not a barrier but a country, and he had finally crossed the border.

That night he lay in his bed and watched the ceiling. Mark was asleep, and the room was quiet. David yawned, tired but content. The year had held an accident, a confession, a journey, and a return; it had held fear and comfort, and more growth than he could measure. He was used to measuring his days by tasks, but some things could not be counted. He thought about his grandmother, who had been active all her life and who now moved slowly, and about Sarah, who had seen him at his most helpless and had stayed. Love, he decided, was not a single feeling but a household of them: patience, courage, and the ordinary work of showing up. A thousand small choices had brought him here, and normal life would ask for a thousand more. He reached for his phone and wrote one line: "I am glad you are in my life." Then he lay back, closed his eyes, and let sleep take him.
