# Chapter 21 · Security Class 安全课堂

> **主题**：讲座、密码、防护

## 正文

On Monday a notice appeared on every dormitory board: students who worked in the offices would attend a security class on Tuesday evening, in the old theater at the eastern end of campus. David had been waiting for something like this since June, when the fake scholarship email had cost several students their savings. He accepted at once. Sarah was in the corridor, pinning up a poster for the exhibition beside the lecture. She had drawn it by hand with a wooden ruler: simple and artistic, a stripe of gray and blue above a photograph of the old industrial district, where an aircraft factory had once stood. "It's a historical exhibition," she said. "Locks, telephones, radios. The university covers eighty acres and has a hundred years of secrets." David took a shot of the poster with his phone. Its beauty, he thought, made a dull subject look like a door left open.

Tuesday came with rain. David met Mark beside the laundry room, where Mark was folding clothes left in the machine since morning. "I always forget them," Mark said. "You know how it is. Twice." They walked along the covered corridor, past the exhibition cases. A retired mechanic was explaining a tin box of wires to a dozen students a few steps away. "A chain is only as strong as its weakest link," he said, tapping the glass, "and a lock is a bargain with physics. It does not care how honest you are." Mark asked whether a thief could pick it with a nail. The mechanic said a nail was the wrong tool, but the question was the right one. Neither of them had anticipated that the exhibition would be interesting; on paper it sounded boring. Outside, the rain roared on the concrete steps, which shone like a slide.

The theater was half full, and the line moved slowly. David and Mark found seats in the middle, behind Sarah, who was saving places. Ms. Chen, a campus security officer and an ambassador for a national safety program, stood on the wooden deck that served as the stage. She did not begin with rules. She asked how many used the same password for everything. A forest of hands went up. "That is the jungle," she said. "One path in, and the wolf can walk everywhere." A slide showed three boxes stacked like a cube: password, device, network. Security, she said, was not one lock but a combination. Reusing a password was an elementary mistake, she added; every system has an edge, and the edge is usually a person. She listed the ten most common passwords, a parade of nonsense: 123456, password, qwerty. The importance of the class, she said, was not fear but habit.

Then she asked for a volunteer. A pale boy from the business school climbed onto the stage and typed a password into a tablet connected to the screen. The word was sunshine. Her colleague ran a program, and the letters fell apart in seconds. "Length beats complexity," Ms. Chen said. "A long sentence you can remember is more durable than a short word you cannot." She held up a magnet and a dead hard drive to demonstrate how easily a laptop could be copied in a cafe. Someone asked about radiation from phones; she said the science did not support the fear, and the truly dangerous thing was simpler: a lost device, a shared charger, a password written on a note. David thought about his June discovery, the fake page's one flaw, and how little it had taken to destroy a stranger's trust.

She told three stories. The first was about a bride who found a loan taken in her name before her wedding; the bank agreed to cancel the card only after she produced a cheque and three forms of identity. The second was about a traveler who lost his passport on the deck of a ferry and spent a week in an office of forms and fees. The third was about a small retail business that tried to market its goods online, ignored a warning email, and watched its financial records disappear in a single night. "None of these people were stupid," she said. "They were busy, and criminals attack the tired." The punishment for the criminals was harsh, she said, but the victims still carried the cost. "You do not want to learn this lesson in hell," she said. "Learn it in a warm hall, with tea."

During the break the corridor was a mix of students and staff, with hot tea, orange cake and lemon water. Sarah had a suggestion. "Let us run a simulation," she said. "Two divisions: one attacks, one defends. It will teach more than a slide." Ms. Chen asked for volunteers to undertake the exercise. David put up his hand; Mark did too, because Sarah had. They were given a mock retail company: an online shop selling clothes, with a cashier page and a customer list. "Your fee for failure is embarrassment," Ms. Chen said. "Your fee for success is tea." David considered the items on the screen and felt his pulse rise. What would motivate a stranger to spend a night attacking a shop that sold shirts? In preparation, the red team was sent to another room, and the blue team had ten minutes to plan.

Their plan was simple. They changed the default password, inserted a second check, and removed a surplus admin page nobody used. Mark wanted to leave a fake trap and "destroy the enemy"; Sarah objected on principle. "We should not interfere with their machines," she said. "That is not defense, it is attack." David made a decision: they would follow the rules and nail down every permission. "Obey the policy," he said. "If we win by breaking it, we learn nothing." The red team sent an urgent message identical to the company's real one, with a link to a login page. A blue team member clicked. The room groaned. "This is an epidemic," Ms. Chen said, "and the vaccine is a habit." They closed the hole, but the mistake had done its work, and the red team announced its next goal: to crash the server.

In the second round, the blue team had to send a genuine message and detect the fake one. David noticed a stripe in the logo that was the wrong shade, and a phone number from no source they could verify. They reported it, and the red team's first attempt failed. Then came the blast: a dozen messages in two minutes, each slightly different, hoping to overwhelm them. The blue team did not panic. "A frequent attack is not a competent one," Sarah said, quoting the lecture. They checked each message against the list, and the red team ran out of ideas. When Ms. Chen called time, the scrape of chairs sounded like applause. Mark said he had never worked so hard for a cup of tea. The difficulty, David realized, was not the technology but staying calm, and possibly the best lesson of the night.

Afterward, Ms. Chen talked about the real world. A hospital where a virus spread through a USB stick; a factory where one email stopped production; a gang that robbed ordinary citizens with stolen identities. "No gun, no blood, no blast," she said. "Just a click." In extreme cases the consequences could be grave: a business could close, a family could lose its savings. David asked whether the law could do more. She said it was tough, but far slower than prevention; from a defensive standpoint, prevention was cheaper than punishment, and privacy was not a luxury. "You cannot wholly remove risk," she said. "You can weigh it, and you can lower it." She did not refer to any victim by name; instead she described her vision of a campus where every student knew the first rule. "Furthermore," she said, "talk to each other. A reliable friend is the best alarm system. If a small shop can flourish, it is because its people look after one another." She handed out cards with a phone number and a request: report anything strange, however small. He put the card beside his student ID, wanting to contribute something, not just listen.

Walking home, David and Sarah stopped at the campus shop. He bought a notebook; the cashier asked if he needed a bag. In the window, a red stocking hung beside winter clothes, and the wet pavement reflected the lights like a long slide. Sarah said she wanted to turn the class into a comic for the student paper. David offered to do the writing. "A proper article," he said. "Not too many adverbs." She laughed and said he should appreciate how rare a good writer was. The rain had stopped, the air soft, and a bicycle passed, its wheels beginning to spin. In the shadow of the library, he felt a warmth he did not try to name. Those feelings, he suspected, would not stay in the outward calm for long. That night, as he often did, he called his mother. She asked about the class and whether he was eating vegetables; he told her about the boy who clicked. "Your father always says a door has two locks," she said. "One on the door, and one in the head." He wrote the sentence down and looked at it for a while, then cooked noodles with pepper sauce and slept well.
