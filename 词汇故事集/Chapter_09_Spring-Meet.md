# Chapter 09 · Spring Meet 春季运动会

> **主题**：训练、比赛、受伤

## 正文

The last snow of winter fell on Haizhou in the first week of March, and by Monday it had melted into gray water along the gutters. David stood at the door of the sports association office, watching the weather and thinking about the schedule. As an organizer, he had been given the regulations, a list of jobs, and a budget already too small. Sarah, who hosted a weekly radio program on the science of sport, had asked him to help with research; Mark, whose football team had finished the winter league in third place, was training twice a day. "You look worried," Mark said. "It is only a sports meeting." "It is a sports meeting with fifteen hundred participants," David said. Mark laughed, but David was increasingly sure that the whole thing would collapse. Instead of an easy spring, he had a schedule, a budget, and a distinct feeling that something would go wrong.

The first problem was equipment. The university's old hurdles and timing clocks were in poor repair, and the coach had asked David to order replacements from a sports agency downtown. He took the subway into the city with Sarah. They could have taken a taxi, but the subway was cheaper. The agency occupied a narrow building near the bus terminal, between a shop selling tropical fruit and a monument. A clerk showed them a catalogue. The prices were dear. "This brand is superior to the ones you have," the clerk said, "and the discount is valid until Friday." David used his calculator to compute the total. Twenty minus four was sixteen; he checked it twice. "Accurate," Sarah said. "My primary concern is not accuracy," he said. "It is whether the association can pay."

The order was large: thirty hurdles, a set of stopwatches, two hundred elastic bandages, and a microphone. The city sports club sent six hurdles on loan. The agency would deliver everything to the port warehouse, where a ship from the south would bring the rest of the equipment in a steel container. It was a bold order, but David signed the form. The manager added a condition. "The cargo must be collected within three days," she said. "After that we charge storage by the ton." David promised to fulfill the order and asked her to insure the shipment. "Of course," she said, "but insurance covers accident, not carelessness." She specified the date and drove away in a small auto. David put a tick beside the date and folded the receipt into his pocket.

The container arrived on a cold morning. David and Mark borrowed the groundskeeper's old station wagon and drove down to the harbor. The port was busy: a cargo ship from the south was unloading, and trucks waited by the terminal gate. The wagon was too small for thirty hurdles, so they packed the bandages and microphones first and sent for a van. They had no radio communication with the campus, so Mark promised to call when they reached the ridge road. Mark, who had grown up on a farm, looked at the water and said it reminded him of the spring crop his father would be planting that week. "The lambing is over," he said. "My father says the birth of a lamb is the best weather report there is." They secured the boxes with rope, and Mark drove up the steep road. From the top they could see the bay, flat and silver under the clouds.

The spring meet needed money, and the association decided to hold a charity sale in the square beside the monument. Sarah, who had taken the initiative, made jam from strawberries and sold it in small jars; Mark, who could cook, chopped vegetables and mixed a dressing with vinegar and oil. David tied a string of balloons above the table. The sale was a small enterprise, but it worked. By noon a crowd had gathered, and customers bargained over everything. A woman bought an umbrella for her son. An older man asked whether the jam contained too much sugar. "A gram of sugar more or less will not hurt you," Sarah said, and he bought two jars. Even the groundskeeper, a man with an explosive temper, stopped to buy a book and left a coin in the tin.

The trouble came at three o'clock. A young man in a gray coat had been standing around the cash box for several minutes, and when Sarah turned to serve a customer, he grabbed the box and ran. "Thief!" she shouted. Mark chased him across the square and down the steps to the subway entrance. A campus guard with a dog on a leather collar joined the chase. The thief was fast, but the guard's dog was faster. It caught his sleeve, and the box fell open on the pavement. The guard was angry, and Sarah, who had run after them, asked him to show mercy; the thief was only a hungry first-year. "Call the police," she said, "but do not hurt him." Later, the association voted to recommend that the boy work at the meet instead of paying a fine, and the university promised to investigate the theft.

The next week was given to training. David signed up for the eight hundred meters. The coach watched him struggle through four laps and then recommended a program of short runs and long walks. She gave them a description of the route and said she would evaluate their progress. "Your muscles and joints are fine," she said. "The problem is that you go too fast too early." She showed him how to adjust his pace and how to ease the pain by breathing slowly. "Skill matters less than patience," she added. "A healthy body needs rest as much as exercise." Temperatures would drop that week, so they ran wherever the surface was flat. Mark's knee began to swell after a heavy football practice, and the nurse wrapped it in an elastic bandage and told him to stay off the field. "Excessive training is worse than no training," she said. "The body needs time for growth." David brought him ice and a pillow for his leg. "You are a handy nurse," Mark said. "Personally, I would rather have a doctor."

On Thursday Sarah recorded her program in the radio room. The microphone they had bought was faulty, and the battery in the recorder kept dying, so David replaced the cell and soldered a loose copper wire to the light bulb. They tested the operation of the timing system. Sarah explained her subject: the psychology of sport. "Children imitate what they see," she said. "If a kindergarten teacher runs, the children run. If she sits, they sit." She interviewed a learned professor who had studied abroad for a year. He spoke about the ratio of speed to endurance and the definition of an amateur. "The plural of 'athlete' is not 'athletes'," he joked, "it is 'patients'." Sarah asked him to repeat the sentence. They computed the heart rate of a runner, checked the diameter of a balloon, and put a tick beside each answer. The program would be broadcast on Friday, in conjunction with the opening of the meet, and Sarah put emphasis on the word science. It was a good question whether sport was science or something else.

Race day was cold but glorious. The whole campus gathered at the stadium. David stood at the start with nine other runners. The runners went to their respective marks. The course went round the track twice and then out through the gate and high up the ridge path, a steep climb; the old track had one disadvantage, but the route on the ridge was dry. At the first lap he was fifth; at the second he was third. Then, near the peak of the climb, his foot caught a stone and he fell. His ankle began to swell at once. Sarah's voice came over the loudspeaker: "Get up. The race is not over." He got up and walked, then ran until the finish line. He was ninth, the worst result of his life, and he did not care. The coach met him at the line. "You finished," she said. "That is what matters."

That evening the association held a small party. Sarah brought the last jar of strawberry jam, and the coach made a brief appearance to praise the organization. The manager sent word that the equipment had arrived and the insurance was still valid; the meet, she said, had been the best in fifteen years. David sat with ice on his ankle and thought about everything since November: the training, the sale, the race, the strange occurrence at the cash box. We had told ourselves the spring would be easy, and we were wrong, he thought, but we had also been lucky. "Next year," Mark said, "you will win." "Maybe," David said. "Or maybe I will just finish without falling." Sarah raised her glass. "To next year," she said. "The victory will be yours." Outside the door the first rain of April began to fall. He had come with a belief that hard work was enough, and the spring had taught him that it was not enough by itself; but he still had faith in it, and hope, and that was not necessarily a bad way to begin the next chapter of his life. The significance of the meet was not the medals but the people.
